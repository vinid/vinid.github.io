Reflecting on 100M Downloads. The Making of a CLIP model

March 16, 2026

FashionCLIP just reached 100M downloads on HuggingFace. Given the milestone, I want to ramble a bit about how FashionCLIP, and the other CLIP models that I have worked have changed my career path.

This is a story of how small projects, done for fun, can snowball into larger, bigger and more impactful projects.

I fine-tuned 3 main CLIPs in the last 6 years and the hard work we put each of these has allowed me and many of my colleagues not only to feel proud about something we put out in the wild, but also to get amazing feedback from the community. This story has multiple characters, without them what nothing of this would have been true. I am but just a small part in the big cog that had to run to produce all of this.

CLIP-Italian

Back in 2021 I was a fresh Postdoc at Bocconi. We were still in COVID-19 times, hopping on public transporation wearing masks and showing vaccination passes to be allowed into buildings. I was already more than one year out of my PhD but somehow the pandemic had reshaped my perception of time and I felt like I had basically just started. I was researching many NLP things: topic modeling, ethics in AI and general language modeling stuff.1 Thanks to Dirk Hovy and his amazing lab, I had lots of freedom to explore any idea I wanted.

One day in what I guess was June, HuggingFace announced a new event: the “HuggingFace Community Week”. Long story short, this was an event in which HF and Google would provide free TPU access and some tech support to train whatever you wanted - as long as you used JAX to do it. The process was rather simple: submit a project proposal on the forum and if approved you are good to go.

Scorlling by the various proposal on the forum, I remember seeing, a post by someone that wanted to train a language-specific CLIP; I don’t exactly remember what language the OP wanted to train it on but my first thought was to do the same thing but for Italian. I knew little about JAX, I didn’t know a thing about CLIP. I mean I saw the news about CLIP, I just didn’t know what it was in practice. However, the code was already provided by the HuggingFace team and they were really active in helping us fix bug and problems; we mostly had to find the data and train.

I shared my idea with my friend Giuseppe, at the time PhD Student in Turin. He was very excited about it (we recoved the slack message where I asked him about this). After posting on the forum, a few people replied and we built a team of amazing folks and we started grinding for two weeks. This was the definition of a small, fun, low stakes summer project.

Putting this together, in just two weeks was not easy, even with all the code already provided. We had to manage random data download from the web, some messed up image-caption mappings, we were also able to translate only a portion of the 3CC dataset because we didn’t have enough API credits. We had to find data from some remote places and websites. We luckly found the very cool WIT dataset that contained Wikipedia images and their respective captions and we extracted the italian subset.2 We then decided to remove all the captions that contained too many entities because we deemed those not useful in learning good features (e.g., the caption “A dog looking at a red flower” is better than “Justin Bieber”). We used this arcane and secret NLP technique called POS-Tagging (now it’s illegal to use it) and removed all the captions that were composed of 80% or more proper nouns.

Then we had to chose which encoders to use for the contrastive training, there were a few options for Italian but we had to basically go with vibes because there was no time for ablation studies: Training took a while to run and we only had some TPUs, so we could not scale horizontally.

It was so much fun! Eventually we were able to put this out in the wild for people to see and we got surprisingly good reactions. The link to the actual project we proposed does not exist anymore, but I have found the link that shows that we were runner-ups for the final awards. CLIP Italian also had a cameo on Yannic Kilcher video newsletter!

We didn’t know what to do with CLIP-Italian, we wrote an arxiv paper and it was kept only on arxiv for two years. Then we decided to submit this to the italian NLP conference and gave this work a little house.

The model we got out of the project was actually pretty good! It might be hard to believe in 2026, but back in 2021 the fact that a simple multi-modal model could retrieve “two dogs on the snow” in Italian was out of the ordinary. Those were the first hints of semantic understanding in embeddings models.3

Why am I telling you about CLIP-Italian? because that was the stepping stone for FashionCLIP.

FashionCLIP

A few years before, I had started collaborating with Jacopo Tagliabue on various projects related to the ecommerce sector.

After he saw CLIP-Italian, he said “what if we do this but for retail” and I of course said “why not”. Jacopo got in contact with Diogo at Farfetch and Diogo found a way to give us access to some of that data that Farfetch was using. Up to this day, that data is one of the cleanest datasets I have ever seen.

Jacopo then introduced me to Patrick, at the time he was his employee, now a close friend of mine. With Patrick I rewrote the training of CLIP and then he ported that to Metaflow and also made it run on multiple GPUs to scale the batch size. We trained this model and it was surprisingly good. Retrieval was so interesting.

However, publishing FashionCLIP was not easy: we submitted to the 2022 NAACL Industry Track but the paper was sadly rejected. Maybe it was not the perfect fit but that was a real bummer.4 I think we were discouraged for a little while but we did not give up. Eventually, we just decided to submit FashionCLIP to Scientific Reports. Scientific Reports tends to accept anything that is well made ignoring the novely factor, so we thought it could be a good venue to close this chapter of our life. After a few months of reviewing, the paper was finally accepted in November 2022.

We were happy with this conclusion and that was it, but we didn’t expect FashionCLIP to be used as much. FashionCLIP is and has been used in Fortune 500 companies and many startups. I met random people in random places and they were excited when I told them I worked on FashionCLIP because they were using it in production. As of March 16th 2026, FashionCLIP is still the second most trending model on the platform.5

I attribute this success to two things: a very permissive licensing and the fully open-source release (easy to use code and easy to download model, with an API compatible with OpenAI’s one): this made the model a drop in replacement for any Fashion pipeline that was using CLIP.

PLIP

This story ends with PLIP a CLIP model for pathology.

I arrived at Stanford in 2022 afer my first two CLIPs were out and I gave a talk on my research to James Zou’s lab during one of the hot days of summer. I remember that it was hot because in my first few days on campus the power went out for 2 or 3 days.

Anyway, a few weeks after from my talk, Zhi Huang (now a very cool Professor at UPenn) reached out to me: he had some cool Twitter data with image-text paired that could be used to train a CLIP model. The data was very cool: it was a collection of 270k tweets from “Pathology Twitter”, full of people describing interesting cases and problems.

Zhi, I and our partner in crime Mert started working on this project. In a few weeks we had our first prototype. This work too was also not so easy: Twitter data is never clean, but this was definetly useful as a proxy to learn good representations of text and images for a very specific medical domain. We had to develop custom classifiers to clean the data and we also had to colelct additional data by sampling pathology data from the famous LAION dataset. After some hard work our final output was a Nature Medicine paper. PLIP is my most cited paper, getting closer to 1000 citations.

As of March 16th 2026, PLIP is the 10th most trending model on the platform, with a total of 5M downloads.
